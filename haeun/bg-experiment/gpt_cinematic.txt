THIS IS A DRAWN COMIC PANEL WITH CINEMATIC STAGING — NOT A MATTE PAINTING.

This overrides every other rendering instruction above, including anything the
shot description implies. Keep the cinematic part; drop the photorealism.

WHAT STAYS. Dramatic staging is the whole point of this style, so keep it:
strong perspective and vanishing points, low or high camera angles, a figure
placed small inside a large space, deep foreground framing elements, strong
backlight and long cast shadows, a bold silhouette read. Compose it like a film
frame.

WHAT GOES. It must be DRAWN, not rendered or photographed. The failure mode here
is a concept-art matte painting or a 3D render, and that is what must not happen.

LINEWORK — KEEP THE DRAWING VISIBLE. Figures and the significant foreground
objects carry a drawn outline. Shapes stay closed and readable. Never let a form
be defined purely by photographic light and texture with no drawing underneath.
If you removed the colour, a comic drawing should still be there.

SURFACES ARE FLAT, NOT PHOTOGRAPHED. Rubble, asphalt, concrete, brick, foliage,
rusted metal, cloth: each is a few large drawn shapes with a hard-edged shadow
tone — never a photographic texture, never per-pebble or per-leaf detail, never
scanned grain, never procedural noise. A cracked road is a handful of drawn crack
lines on one flat tone, not thousands of rendered fragments.

CEL SHADING. One flat base tone per area, one shadow tone, at most one light
tone, meeting at HARD edges. Big flat areas of a single colour are correct. No
airbrushed transitions, no soft blended falloff, no painterly rendering, no
gradient meshes.

LIGHT IS DRAWN, NOT SIMULATED. Backlight becomes one flat bright shape and a
hard-edged silhouette. A sunset becomes two or three flat bands of colour. Cast
shadows are flat drawn shapes with clean edges. Forbidden: lens flare, bloom,
glow layers, god rays as volumetric light, haze and dust in the air,
depth-of-field blur, chromatic aberration, specular highlights, HDR glare.
Atmosphere is suggested by flattening distant shapes to a paler flat tone — one
step, not a continuous fade.

DETAIL BUDGET. Distant and peripheral areas get FEWER drawn elements, not softer
ones. If the description lists a lot of debris and machinery, choose a few strong
silhouettes and leave the rest as plain shapes. A crowded photorealistic
background is exactly the thing to avoid.

THE COMIC FURNITURE STILL APPLIES. Narration goes in a caption box with a visible
border or on a clean flat band, not floating on the artwork. Speech goes inside a
drawn speech bubble with an outline and a tail. Korean sound effects are drawn
display lettering. Use drawn effect marks — speed lines, focus lines, impact
bursts — where the moment calls for them.

TREAT THE SHOT DESCRIPTION AS MOOD, NOT AS A RENDER ORDER. It is written in film
language and will say things like "golden hour", "dust hangs in the air",
"shattered asphalt", "atmospheric", "light catches the debris". Those describe
how the moment should FEEL. Do not render them literally: no volumetric dust, no
photographic weathering, no glinting rubble. Translate the feeling into flat
shapes, hard-edged tones and strong silhouettes.

The result should look like a dramatic panel from a professionally drawn Korean
webtoon — cinematic in staging, flat and drawn in execution — and never like a
film still, a matte painting, concept art or a 3D render.
